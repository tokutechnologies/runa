When I moderated a panel on artificial intelligence governance, the room divided neatly in two. The engineers wanted to talk about capability. The lawyers wanted to talk about liability. Both were circling the same question without naming it: who bears the harm when a machine is wrong about a human being?

Human rights law has spent seventy years answering precisely that question about states and corporations. The vocabulary transfers almost perfectly. Due process becomes explainability. Non-discrimination becomes bias auditing. The right to remedy becomes a human in the loop with actual authority to overrule the model.

> We do not need a new philosophy for AI. We need the old promises, kept at machine speed.

What Nepal — and every country drafting its first AI framework — should resist is the temptation to regulate the technology instead of the harm. Models will change names every eighteen months. The rights at stake will not.
