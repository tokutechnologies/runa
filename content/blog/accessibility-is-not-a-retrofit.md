The first thing you notice, if you are looking, is the step. One concrete step at the entrance of a government office — fifteen centimetres that decide whether a citizen in a wheelchair petitions the state in person or sends someone else to be a citizen on their behalf. Nepal's law, on paper, abolished that step years ago. The concrete has not read the statute.

The Act Relating to Rights of Persons with Disabilities, the Constitution's equality guarantees, and the CRPD — which Nepal ratified without reservation — together promise something quite radical: that access to buildings, to information, to transport and to justice is not a courtesy extended to some citizens, but a right held by all of them. The legal architecture is genuinely good. The physical architecture is where the appeal gets dismissed.

> A ramp added after the ribbon-cutting is not inclusion. It is an apology, poured in concrete.

##### The holding

Accessibility fails in Nepal not for want of law but for want of sequence. We legislate, we inaugurate, and only then — if a complaint lands, if a monitor visits, if a budget line survives — do we retrofit. The right is treated as a finishing touch. But rights are load-bearing. They belong in the drawings, in the procurement rules, in the inspection checklist that decides whether a building may open at all.

##### The remedy

Three moves would change the sequence. Make accessibility certification a condition of occupancy, not a recommendation after it. Fund the retrofit of courts and service offices first — the places where rights are claimed. And count: no ministry should be able to say how many buildings it owns without saying how many a wheelchair can enter. What gets measured gets ramped.

The step outside that office is small. So is a signature on a ratification instrument. The distance between the two is where this work lives.
