Every detention facility keeps a ledger, and every ledger tells two stories. The first is written in columns: name, age, date of admission, alleged offence, next date of hearing. The second is written in what the columns cannot hold.

A monitor learns to read both. The written story says a man was admitted on the fourth. The unwritten one asks why his medical examination is dated the ninth. The written story counts eleven women in a ward built for six. The unwritten one is the temperature of that room at two in the afternoon, which no column records.

> The registry is the institution's memory. Our job is to notice what it has chosen to forget.

Prison monitoring is often imagined as dramatic — surprise inspections, confrontations, revelations. Mostly it is arithmetic and patience. You count mattresses. You compare the kitchen's ration book against the census. You ask the same quiet question in three different rooms and note whether the answers agree.

And then you write your own ledger: the report. It will have columns too, and recommendations, and a date. What keeps the work honest is remembering that behind every row is a person who cannot leave the page.
