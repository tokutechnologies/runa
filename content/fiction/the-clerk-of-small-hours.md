The courthouse emptied the way a tide goes out — first the litigants, then the lawyers, then the light. Bishnu stayed. He was the clerk of small hours, the one who squared the day's petitions into their folders after everyone whose names were in them had gone home to worry.

The last petition of the day had no lawyer's stamp, no fee ticket, no thumbprint. Just careful handwriting: *My son was taken on the night of Falgun 12. I am told there is no record. I request the court to make one.*

Bishnu knew the rule. No fee, no filing. He also knew the archive — four rooms of string-bound bundles where the country kept its arguments with itself. Somewhere in there, he was sure, absence had a shelf.

He carried the petition down the corridor, past the retired ceiling fans, and stood in the archive doorway with his lantern of a phone. The bundles breathed dust. He had filed ten thousand papers in that room and had never once read one for himself.

He read this one again. Then he did the only lawless thing of his careful life: he opened the register for Falgun of that year, found the blank line where a record should have been, and wrote the boy's name into it in his best hand — *entry pending, matter alive.*

It was not a remedy. It was not even, strictly, legal. But when the mother returned in the morning, there would be a line in a government book that admitted her son had existed. Bishnu locked the archive and went home through the small hours, feeling the specific weight of one sheet of paper, which is nothing, and is everything.
