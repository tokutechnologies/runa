On her last day, the justice asked the bench clerk for the cause list and received eleven files. She read twelve.

The twelfth was not on any list. It was the file she had carried in her head for thirty-one years — the one from her first month, when she was too junior to dissent and too frightened to say so. She had signed with the majority. The majority had been wrong.

There is no procedure for reopening a case that exists only in a judge's conscience. So she did what the law allows everyone, even judges: she wrote a petition. To whom it may concern. Re: the matter I got wrong.

She set out the facts, the holding, and the harm. She ruled against herself in careful paragraphs. As relief, she directed the respondent — one retired justice, address known — to spend her remaining years saying so, out loud, to anyone learning the law.

Then she granted the petition, sealed nothing, and left the file on the bench for the morning, where the light would find it first.
